Early findings

Patients and chatbots

People who use a chatbot instead of care stand out for trust, much more than for access.

In a 2025 Canadian survey, 42% of about 6,200 health-chatbot users said they had used chatbot advice instead of contacting a health professional. Having no regular provider made little difference (−2.7 points, 90% CI −5.9 to 0.5). People unhappy with how soon or how close care was scored 4.0 and 6.2 points higher. People with high trust in chatbot health information scored 34.4 points higher (95% CI 31.7 to 37.0). These are survey associations, so they don't show what care people gave up.

Working paper · 2025 Canadian Digital Health Survey

Testing AI scribes

Two recordings aren't enough to pick an AI scribe.

I re-analysed three deployed scribes on 141 public or simulated consultations. A two-recording test picked the best one 52.3% of the time and the worst one 23.8% of the time. Picking at random finds the best one 33.3% of the time. It took 20 recordings to reach 90%. The findings are verified errors on benchmark notes, so they aren't patient harms.

Working paper · Public and simulated benchmark data

Testing the testers

An accuracy test means little until you know how accurate the reviewers are.

Take a 25-note pass or fail test of a tool with 30% bad notes. If the reviewer catches every error, the tool passes about 9% of the time. If the reviewer catches 60%, it passes about 52% of the time. A test that holds up when reviewer accuracy is only known to lie in a range takes 732 notes instead of 25. Measuring reviewers once with known-answer notes removes much of that cost.

Patients and consent

About 1 in 8 Ontarians told about an AI scribe recall no explicit consent.

In a Canadian online panel, 12.8% of Ontario respondents told that their provider used an AI scribe recalled only implied consent or never being asked (95% interval 10.1 to 15.4). Nationally it was 13.2%, and the share was highest at age 75 and over, at 27.8%, with a wide interval.

Working paper · 2025 Canadian Digital Health Survey

Paying for AI

Saving a physician's time can lower their pay.

In a worked scenario, five saved charting hours a month, used to finish earlier, cost CAD 650 in British Columbia, CAD 577.50 in Alberta and CAD 400 in Ontario. US Medicare pay doesn't change when service levels follow medical decision making. Using the time for added care raises pay in every setting, before costs. I haven't confirmed how these rules apply to any one physician's contract.
