Patients and chatbots

People without a regular provider showed no evidence of turning to chatbots instead of a professional more often.

In the 2025 Canadian Digital Health Survey, an online panel run in early 2026, 42% of about 6,200 people who had used general AI chatbots for health said they had used one to seek medical advice or guidance instead of contacting a health professional. Having no regular provider, compared with having a family doctor or nurse practitioner, showed no evidence of a higher rate (−2.7 points, 90% CI −5.9 to 0.5, under the survey's own weights). People unhappy with how soon or how close care was were 4.0 and 6.2 percentage points more likely to report doing so, after adjustment. People with high trust in chatbot health information were 34.4 points more likely (95% CI 31.7 to 37.0), though a gap like that is expected, since the trust question and the outcome are about the same chatbot advice. People in the top third of self-rated eHealth literacy were 14.6 points more likely than those in the bottom third (95% CI 11.3 to 18.0), about half that (7.4) in a later check holding the other measures fixed. Trust and access are measured differently, so these numbers don't rank one above the other. People who did this were also more likely, not less, to report professional care in the past year: any walk-in or non-regular GP contact (10.4 points, 95% CI 7.7 to 13.1), emergency or urgent-care contact (7.9, 5.4 to 10.4) and regular-provider contact (3.3, 1.2 to 5.4). These are survey associations: they don't show why people turned to chatbots, or that chatbots replaced any care.

Testing AI scribes

Two recordings aren't enough to pick an AI scribe.

I re-analysed three deployed scribes on 141 public or simulated consultations. A two-recording test picked the best one 52.3% of the time and the worst one 23.8% of the time. Picking at random finds the best one 33.3% of the time. It took 20 recordings to reach 90%, and 30 when only critical errors count. A panel of AI models, not clinicians, confirmed the errors on benchmark notes, so they aren't patient harms.

Testing the testers

Not knowing how accurate the reviewers are can cost more than having inaccurate ones.

Take the textbook test for error-free review: pass a tool if at most 4 of 25 notes are flagged. A tool with 30% bad notes passes about 9% of the time when review is error-free, with every error caught and no false alarms. If the reviewer catches 60% of errors and raises no false alarms, it passes about 52% of the time. To tell a tool with 10% bad notes from one with 30%, keeping each wrong decision to 10% or less, a test needs 25 notes with error-free review, and at most 75 when the reviewer's catch and false-alarm rates are known, anywhere within 60–100% and 0–5%. If you only know the rates lie somewhere in that range, it needs 732. Measuring a reviewer team once, with a bank of notes whose errors are already known, brings the expected cost to 139 notes per product, measuring notes included (though not the cost of making them), or 65 per product across 20 products, if the team catches 97% of errors with 0.5% false alarms. Each test still holds both risks to 10%; counting the small chance that the measurement itself is off, each overall risk is at most 15%. With a measuring set chosen for the worst case on the paper's accuracy grid, it's about 352 and 179. The model assumes independent notes and reviewers who are equally accurate on every tool, and the 60–100% range is an illustration: the few measured catch rates the paper cites are lower, about 25–37%, for physicians editing drafts and AI judges rather than dedicated reviewers.

Patients and consent

About 1 in 8 Ontario respondents told about an AI scribe recall only implied consent to AI tools, or never being asked.

In a Canadian online panel, 12.8% of Ontario respondents who had been told their provider used an AI scribe said that, when asked to consent to AI tools, they typically gave only implied consent or were never asked (exploratory subset; conditional 95% interval 10.1 to 15.4). The question covers AI tools in general, not one visit, and the answers are recalled, not verified. Nationally it was 13.2%, and the share was highest at age 75 and over, at 27.8%, with a wide interval.

Paying for AI

Saving a physician's time can lower their pay.

In a worked scenario, five saved charting hours a month, used to finish earlier, cost CAD 650 under British Columbia's Longitudinal Family Physician model, CAD 577.50 under Alberta's Primary Care Physician Compensation Model and CAD 400 under Ontario's Family Health Organization Plus (less if the physician already logs a lot of indirect-care time), at their printed base rates. US Medicare pay doesn't change when service levels follow medical decision making. In the paper's schedule, using the time for extra completed, payable services raises pay in every setting, before costs. I haven't confirmed how these rules apply to any one physician's contract.

Hospital records systems

Epic is the main record system at 46% of matched US hospitals, and 37% of critical access hospitals.

This is each hospital's main inpatient EHR, worked out by a written rule from ONC's public 2024 file. Of 4,054 hospitals that could be matched, Epic was the main system at 46.4%, Oracle Health at 20.8%, MEDITECH at 16.5% and TruBridge at 7.4%. At critical access hospitals, Epic's share was 36.7% and TruBridge's 15.5%. In Texas, 194 of 300 matched hospitals reported no Epic inpatient EHR. This shows what hospitals reported to Medicare's Promoting Interoperability program, not a full install base. The main-EHR call comes from that rule, not from a field in ONC's file. Of the 4,627 hospitals, 569 had no linked product and 4 reported only add-ons.

AI transparency rules

53 of 216 EHR developers linked a public AI risk summary. The US duty to publish these summaries is ending.

On 26 Oct 2026, 53 of the 216 developers certified for decision support in ONC's CHPL database linked a public summary of how they manage AI risk. The other 163 stated that they don't currently supply a predictive decision-support tool as part of their certified software. ONC's HTI-5 final rule (91 FR 67916, published 28 Oct 2026) drops that duty from 28 Dec 2026. The 52 linked addresses (51 once a trailing slash is ignored) were recorded on 26 Oct, so a re-check from January can show which stay online. This is a count on one day. Each "not currently supplied" answer is the developer's own statement about its certified software, and other parties' tools can still run inside it.
